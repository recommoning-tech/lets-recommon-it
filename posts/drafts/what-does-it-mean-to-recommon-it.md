## The tools we depend on belong to someone else

Most of us run our lives through systems we don't own and can't change. Our photos, messages, calendars, and documents sit on servers owned by a handful of companies. The rules are written in terms of service we never negotiated. When a company changes those rules, raises prices, or shuts a service down, we adapt or leave. Leaving usually means losing our data and our connections.

This is not an accident or a temporary phase. It is how the business works.

## This has happened before

The word *enclosure* comes from English history. Over several centuries, land that villagers had used in common, for grazing, firewood, and food, was fenced off and turned into private property. Commoners lost not only resources but a way of life and a say in how it was run. Silvia Federici and Peter Linebaugh both show that enclosure was not a single event. It is a process that repeats whenever something shared becomes valuable enough to take.

The early internet and free software were built in a commons spirit. Protocols were open, code was shared, and anyone could run a server. Giorgio Griziotti describes how that collective intelligence was gradually captured by platforms that turned our attention, relationships, and knowledge into raw material. Generative AI continues the pattern. It is trained on the shared record of human writing and art, then sold back to us through a few companies that control the hardware.

## Commoning is a verb

Linebaugh puts it simply: there is no commons without commoning. A commons is not just a resource. It is a group of people who share something and the practices they use to look after it together.

That is what *recommon* means here: building the practices, skills, and institutions that let communities hold their information technology in common again. It is not just "use open source" or "self-host your apps." Those can help, but on their own they can remain individual projects. One person's homelab doesn't recommon anything.

## The right scale

Ivan Illich argued that tools past a certain size stop serving people and start controlling them. He called for *convivial* tools that people can understand, use, and adapt for themselves.

This blog works at three scales:

- **Personal:** learning the skills. Running a server, understanding a network.
- **Household:** sharing with the people you live with. Family photos, shared calendars, a password manager.
- **Community:** a neighborhood, a club, a co-op, a small organization. This is where the hard and interesting problems are, because now the system belongs to more than one person.

Each step up adds people, and with people come questions about who decides.

## Owning the hardware is not enough

A community server can be run as badly as any platform. If one person holds every password, makes every decision, and does all the work, that is a small fiefdom with a friendly ruler. When that person burns out or moves away, the whole thing collapses.

Elinor Ostrom spent her career studying how communities govern shared resources. She showed that people can and do manage commons well, but only with the right institutions: rules about who belongs, who can do what, how decisions are made, and how disputes are settled. Her Institutional Analysis and Development framework gives us a way to look at any shared system and ask: who participates, what positions exist, and what rules apply at each level?

That is the second half of this blog. For every technical guide, there is a governance question.

## What you'll find here

- **Guides:** step-by-step instructions for servers, Docker stacks, private networks, and self-hosted services, written for people who are comfortable with a terminal but aren't professional sysadmins.
- **Governance companions:** each guide comes with a post on running that piece of infrastructure as a group, drawing on Ostrom, Bookchin, Federici, and others.
- **Commentary and reviews:** on Big Tech, AI, and the ideas behind community technology.
- **A wiki:** a growing *pattern language for community tech*, in the style of Christopher Alexander, plus an annotated bibliography.
